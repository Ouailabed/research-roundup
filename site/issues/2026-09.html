This Time Is (Mostly) Different: U.S. Tightening Cycles and Global Resilience in the 1980s vs 2020s

Drishan Banerjee, Galina Hale, Harrison Shieh
NBER, 1 September 2026

The paper asks whether the post-pandemic tightening cycle in the United States could repeat the 1982 sudden stop in capital flows to emerging markets. Using a Mundell-Fleming model with a fiscal-space constraint and cross-country data, the authors identify three stylised facts that differentiate the 1980s from the 2020s: monetary policy effectiveness, fiscal space, and external vulnerabilities. They show that fiscal space is a key channel through which higher foreign-currency risk-free rates affect economic performance after tightening. As of 2026 no global recession has followed, and cross-country evidence confirms that fiscal space shapes economic performance after tightening.

Key takeaways

  • The model predicts a central role for fiscal space in transmitting increases in foreign-currency risk-free rates.
  • Monetary policy effectiveness and external vulnerabilities differ markedly between the 1980s and 2020s.
  • Cross-country evidence confirms that fiscal space shapes economic performance after tightening.

Why it matters: Understanding how U.S. rate hikes propagate globally helps the markets team assess currency risk, emerging-market debt exposure, and the likelihood of a synchronized slowdown, informing investment and risk-management decisions.

Tagged Transversal: The abstract studies U.S. monetary tightening and its impact on emerging economies, employing a cross-country framework.

Equilibrium Yield Curves and the Interest Rate Lower Bound

Taisuke Nakata, Hiroatsu Tanaka
Federal Reserve Board, 22 September 2026

The paper asks how an occasionally binding effective lower bound (ELB) influences the shape of yield curves. It builds a calibrated DSGE model for the United States that matches macroeconomic data and the term structure of interest rates. The model finds that the ELB creates state-dependent term premiums that are generally smaller, and that forward guidance at the ELB can raise or lower premiums depending on whether demand or supply shocks dominate.

Key takeaways

  • The ELB induces state-dependent term premiums, typically reducing their size.
  • Forward guidance at the ELB can increase or decrease term premiums depending on the dominant shock type.
  • The calibrated DSGE model reproduces key U.S. macro and yield-curve features, underscoring the ELB's importance for policy analysis.

Why it matters: Understanding how the ELB and forward guidance affect term premiums and yield curves helps forecast bond yields, price interest-rate risk, and assess the impact of central-bank policy on financial markets.

Tagged DM: The abstract calibrates the model to the United States, an advanced economy.

Expectations and the Term Structure of Interest Rates

Pooya Molavi, Alireza Tahbaz-Salehi, Andrea Vedolin
NBER, 22 September 2026

The paper examines how subjective expectations shape the term structure of interest rates, allowing for arbitrary belief formation. It derives a universal condition linking expected and realized rates and uses it to decompose long-term yield sensitivity into expectation and risk-premia components. The authors find that for short- and medium-maturity bonds, movements are driven mainly by changes in expectations rather than risk premia, and they document systematic wedges between expectations of yields across different maturities, indicating inconsistencies in subjective forecasts.

Key takeaways

  • A universal condition linking expected and realized interest rates holds regardless of how expectations are formed
  • Yield changes for short- and medium-term bonds are primarily driven by expectation shifts, not risk premia
  • Systematic wedges between expectations of long-term and short-term yields reveal inconsistencies across maturities

Why it matters: Understanding whether yield movements stem from expectation changes or risk premia helps the markets team forecast interest-rate paths, price bonds, and assess the impact of monetary-policy signals on various segments of the fixed-income market.

Tagged Transversal: The abstract develops a general theory of expectations and term structure without specifying any country or region.

Globalization, Inflation Dynamics and the Slope of the Phillips Curve

Colin J. Hottman, Ricardo M. Reyes-Heroles
Dallas Fed, 22 September 2026

The paper asks how international trade has shaped U.S. inflation and the slope of the Phillips curve. Using a multi-region New Keynesian model, the authors build state-level measures of import penetration and estimate regional Phillips curves for 1976-2017. They find that greater import exposure leads to flatter state-level Phillips curves and accounts for roughly 40 percent of the flattening of the U.S. Phillips curve over this period. It also reduced inflation by about 0.17 percentage point annually between 1976 and 2008. A reversal of globalization to early-1990s levels would reduce the sacrifice ratio by almost one-half.

Key takeaways

  • Greater import exposure significantly flattens state-level Phillips curves in the United States.
  • Rising import exposure accounts for roughly 40 percent of the flattening of the U.S. Phillips curve over 1976-2017.
  • A reversal of globalization to early-1990s levels would reduce the sacrifice ratio by almost one-half, substantially altering monetary policy trade-offs.

Why it matters: Understanding how trade-driven flattening of the Phillips curve affects inflation dynamics helps central banks and investors gauge the effectiveness of monetary policy, forecast inflation trends, and assess the impact of changes in globalization on interest-rate expectations.

Tagged DM: The paper studies inflation dynamics in the United States, an advanced economy.

Pricing Risk Globally: Intermediary Constraints, the Dollar, and the Global Financial Cycle

Ozge Akinci, Ṣebnem Kalemli-Özcan, Albert Queraltó
Federal Reserve Board, 8 September 2026

The paper asks how heightened uncertainty about U.S. asset returns feeds through to global asset prices and exchange rates. Using a two-country model with intermediary balance-sheet constraints and an empirical analysis of uncertainty shocks, the authors show that such shocks widen global credit spreads, lift the dollar and increase currency risk premia. The model explains these movements by tighter intermediary constraints that make net worth valuable in bad times, leading to lower asset prices. The results match observed responses of credit spreads, the dollar and currency premiums.

Key takeaways

  • Uncertainty shocks about U.S. asset returns widen global credit spreads.
  • These shocks appreciate the dollar and raise currency risk premia.
  • Intermediary balance-sheet constraints amplify the effects, lowering asset prices in the model.

Why it matters: Understanding how U.S. uncertainty shocks affect the dollar, credit spreads and currency risk premia helps the markets team price risk, forecast asset-price movements and assess the impact of global financial cycle dynamics on portfolios.

Tagged Transversal: The paper studies uncertainty about U.S. asset returns and its impact on global credit spreads, the dollar and currency risk premia.

Maritime chokepoints and the global economy: evidence from the Strait of Hormuz

Enisse Kharroubi
BIS, 17 September 2026

The paper asks how traffic disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz affect the global economy. It uses historical maritime traffic data from the IMF/University of Oxford Portwatch database to estimate the impact of chokepoint shocks. The analysis finds that commodity prices, especially energy prices, tend to increase after an adverse shock, and that such shocks are followed by lower global output and higher global consumer prices. Financial conditions tighten, with spreads widening on advanced economy corporate bonds and emerging market sovereign bonds.

Key takeaways

  • Commodity prices, especially energy prices, tend to increase after an adverse shock to traffic in the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Negative shocks lead to lower global output and higher consumer inflation, creating stagflation pressures.
  • Financial conditions tighten, with wider spreads on advanced economies corporate bonds and emerging market sovereign bonds.

Why it matters: Understanding how chokepoint shocks affect commodity prices, inflation, and credit spreads helps the markets team assess risks to rates, currencies, and credit markets and inform trading and risk-management decisions.

Tagged Transversal: The abstract studies global economic and financial consequences of disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, affecting both advanced and emerging economies.

Systemic at Home: the Persistence of a Too-Big-to-Fail Premium in Europe

Laura Deen, Daniel Dimitrov
De Nederlandsche Bank, September 2026

The authors quantify the implicit too-big-to-fail funding advantage of large European banks using CDS market data. Using a reduced-form asset-pricing model they separate credit-risk components from a residual TBTF wedge. They find that banks whose assets exceed half of home-country GDP enjoy at least 30% lower credit spreads than comparable peers, and that this wedge persists through 2024 and declines when home-sovereign CDS spreads rise.

Key takeaways

  • Banks whose assets exceed half of home-country GDP enjoy at least 30% lower credit spreads than comparable peers.
  • The TBTF wedge persists through 2024, and the implicit guarantee's share of total spreads has not diminished in recent years.
  • The size of the TBTF premium is linked to sovereign fiscal strength, falling when sovereign CDS spreads rise.

Why it matters: Understanding the persistent TBTF funding advantage helps market participants assess bank credit risk, sovereign-bank linkages, and the potential impact of policy actions on funding conditions in advanced economies.

Tagged DM: The paper studies large European banks, which are based in advanced economies.

Bringing Work Home: Flexible Work Arrangements as Gateway Jobs for Women in West Bengal

Lisa Ho, Suhani Jalota, Anahita Karandikar
Quarterly Journal of Economics, 18 September 2026

The paper examines how flexible work-from-home jobs can draw women in West Bengal, India, who are out of the labor force, into paid employment. Using a randomized experiment with 1,670 households and a jobs platform offering short-term data work, the authors find that job flexibility more than triples take-up, from 15% to 48%, and that trying paid work from home increases later take-up of less flexible jobs among women without previous work experience. Working from the office increases accuracy by 3% and speed by 18%, so remote work comes with trade-offs in worker performance.

Key takeaways

  • Flexible work-from-home jobs are highly effective at bringing women into paid work.
  • Trying paid work from home increases later take-up of less flexible jobs among women without previous work experience, and work experience shifts attitudes to become less traditional.
  • Remote work lowers accuracy and speed, but the expanded labor pool may offset the productivity loss.

Why it matters: Understanding how flexible work can unlock a large untapped female labor force in emerging economies helps assess growth potential, consumer demand, and informs policy or corporate strategies targeting labor market participation.

Tagged EM: The abstract studies women in West Bengal, India, an emerging economy.
