Are Markups Too High? Competition, Strategic Innovation, and Industry Dynamics

Laurent Cavenaile, Murat Alp Celik, Xu Tian
Review of Economic Studies, 6 October 2026

The paper builds a new oligopolistic general‑equilibrium Schumpeterian growth model that links endogenous growth, Cournot competition and dynamic industrial organization. It calibrates the model to US data since the 1970s and shows how a rise in markups, concentration and R&D spending co‑occur with slower productivity and a declining labour share. Simulations indicate that higher markups reduce static welfare but are more than offset by dynamic gains from greater innovation driven by higher profit opportunities. The main driver of weak productivity growth is found to be rising innovation costs.

Key takeaways

  • Higher markups generate a static welfare loss but are outweighed by dynamic welfare gains from increased innovation.
  • Innovation costs have risen, making ideas harder to find and limiting productivity growth.
  • The model replicates US trends such as superstar firm dominance, higher concentration, rising markups and a falling labour share.

Why it matters: Understanding the trade‑off between higher markups and innovation‑driven growth helps forecast corporate earnings, inflation pressure and productivity trends, all of which affect interest rates, equity valuations and credit risk for investors in advanced economies.

Tagged DM: The abstract calibrates the model to US data, an advanced economy.

The Inflation Attention Threshold and Inflation Surges

Oliver Pfäuti
American Economic Review, 30 October 2026

The paper asks when inflation captures public attention and how that alters macro outcomes. Using macroeconomic data, the author estimates an attention threshold at 4 percent inflation, finding that attention doubles above that level. A structural model shows that higher attention amplifies and prolongs the impact of supply shocks on inflation. The framework jointly explains recent inflation surges, the tight co‑movement with expectations, and the long tail of disinflation.

Key takeaways

  • There is an attention threshold at 4% inflation where public attention doubles.
  • Above the threshold, supply shocks have stronger and more persistent effects on inflation.
  • Including attention shifts helps explain inflation surges, the link with expectations, and the prolonged disinflation tail.

Why it matters: Understanding the attention threshold helps forecast inflation dynamics, shape expectations, and anticipate central‑bank policy moves that affect rates, currencies and credit markets.

Tagged Transversal: The abstract develops a macroeconomic theory of inflation attention without referencing any specific country or region.

The Information Effect, Policy Rule Ambiguity, and the Decomposition of Monetary Policy Surprises

Chengcheng Jia
Cleveland Fed, 5 November 2026

The paper asks how imperfect information about economic shocks and about the central bank's policy rule affect monetary policy. Using a New Keynesian model with both types of uncertainty, the author derives optimal policy and calibrates the model to the United States. The calibration shows that the interaction between shock uncertainty and rule ambiguity dominates the variance of monetary policy surprises, accounting for more than 70 percent. The results imply that conventional certainty‑equivalence policies may be inappropriate.

Key takeaways

  • Imperfect information about shocks and the policy rule interact, creating a large interaction effect.
  • Optimal policy is less responsive to shocks and depends on rule ambiguity, breaking certainty equivalence.
  • In the US calibration the interaction effect explains over 70% of the variance of monetary policy surprises.

Why it matters: Understanding the drivers of monetary policy surprises helps forecast interest‑rate moves and asset‑price volatility. The finding that most surprise variance stems from the interaction of shock uncertainty and rule ambiguity highlights the importance of monitoring both macro data uncertainty and central‑bank communication clarity, improving rate‑expectation models and risk management for markets research.

Tagged DM: The model is calibrated to the US economy, an advanced market.

A Model of U.S. Monetary Policy and the Global Financial Cycle

Rohan Kekre, Moritz Lenel
NBER, 20 October 2026

The paper asks how U.S. monetary policy transmits to the global financial cycle. It builds a general-equilibrium model where currency-mismatched arbitrageurs hold mean-variance efficient portfolios and are net short the dollar. The model shows that a U.S. tightening reduces arbitrageurs’ net wealth and raises global risk premia. This mechanism links Fed actions to worldwide asset prices.

Key takeaways

  • U.S. tightening erodes the net wealth of currency‑mismatched arbitrageurs.
  • The wealth loss translates into higher global risk premia.
  • The model provides a channel through which Fed policy affects worldwide asset pricing.

Why it matters: Understanding how Fed actions influence global risk premia helps the markets team forecast movements in equities, bonds, and currencies across regions.

Tagged DM: The paper studies U.S. monetary policy, an advanced economy.

Strategic Monetary Policy in Open Economies

Louphou Coulibaly, Charles Engel
NBER, 27 October 2026

The paper asks how a self‑oriented central bank should set policy in an open economy when financial markets are complete and goods are priced to market. It uses a two‑country New Keynesian model with state‑contingent claims to compare full versus partial commitment regimes. It finds that under full commitment the optimal rule coincides with cooperative policy because asset price adjustments neutralize terms‑of‑trade gains, and that partial commitment leads to a Nash equilibrium with lower welfare.

Key takeaways

  • With complete financial markets, full‑commitment strategic monetary policy yields the same optimal rule as cooperative policy.
  • State‑contingent claim prices offset any welfare gains a self‑interested policymaker could obtain by manipulating terms of trade.
  • Under partial commitment, the resulting Nash equilibrium is welfare‑reducing for the countries involved.

Why it matters: The findings clarify how strategic interactions and commitment choices affect exchange rates, asset valuations and central bank behavior, helping market analysts forecast currency movements and sovereign bond risks.

Tagged Transversal: The abstract presents a generic two‑country model without naming any specific country or region.

Beyond "Looking Through": Monetary Policy Responses and Communication Under Supply-Driven Inflation

Olena Kostyshyna, Luba Petersen, Jing Yang
Bank of Canada, 16 October 2026

The paper investigates how different monetary policy rules and communication strategies affect inflation, output gaps and expectations during persistent supply‑driven shocks. Using a controlled macroeconomic experiment, the authors compare a Lean‑Against rule that reacts strongly to inflation with a Look‑Through rule that reacts less strongly, and test three communication protocols ranging from qualitative statements to detailed projections. Results show that Look‑Through policies raise and prolong inflation unless accompanied by credible forward projections, while detailed quantitative information alone has little effect. The study highlights the importance of projection‑based communication for anchoring expectations and limiting inflation and output losses.

Key takeaways

  • Look‑Through policies increase and prolong inflation under persistent cost‑push shocks, whereas Lean‑Against policies curb inflation more quickly.
  • Providing detailed quantitative information alone does not affect outcomes, but forward projections anchor expectations and lower inflation and output losses.
  • Effective communication of credible projections is essential for making Look‑Through policies viable during supply‑driven inflation.

Why it matters: Understanding how policy stance and communication shape inflation expectations helps markets forecast central‑bank actions, price bonds, assess currency risk and evaluate the likely path of interest rates.

Tagged DM: The research is conducted by the Bank of Canada, an advanced‑economy central bank.

Effectiveness of Monetary Policy Transmission in Morocco amid Reforms to the Monetary Policy Framework

Nadia Mounir, Hannah Brown
IMF, 23 October 2026

The paper evaluates how effective Morocco's monetary policy transmission has been since 2018 after a series of framework reforms. It uses a two‑step empirical strategy, first identifying exogenous policy shocks with a Taylor‑rule‑based approach and then estimating dynamic effects via local projections. The results show that transmission to output and inflation has strengthened post‑reform, with a 1 percentage‑point rate hike reducing output by about 0.3 percent and inflation by 0.1 percent, though effects remain delayed and muted relative to other emerging markets.

Key takeaways

  • Transmission to output and inflation has improved after the 2018 reforms but remains slower and smaller than in many emerging markets.
  • The interest‑rate channel is most active, the exchange‑rate channel has strengthened, while credit, asset‑price and expectations channels are still weak.
  • The findings highlight ongoing challenges for Morocco’s move toward an inflation‑targeting regime, affecting forecasts of inflation, growth and sovereign risk.

Why it matters: Understanding how monetary policy changes affect output and inflation in Morocco helps the research team forecast inflation, assess the impact of rate moves on the local economy and currency, and evaluate sovereign risk and investment opportunities in a key North African emerging market.

Tagged EM: The paper studies monetary policy transmission in Morocco, an emerging economy.

Macroeconomic Effects of Forced Displacement in Sub-Saharan Africa

Hany Abdel-Latif, Antonio David, Mouhamadou Moustapha Ly, Elisee Wendlassida Miningou, Masateru Okamoto, Alex Vaval Pierre-Charles
IMF, 24 October 2026

The paper investigates how forced displacement affects host economies in Sub‑Saharan Africa using a difference‑in‑differences local‑projection panel and detailed case studies of Burkina Faso, South Sudan and Uganda. It finds that large refugee inflows do not materially change host‑country GDP, inflation or the current‑account balance over a five‑year horizon, and that fiscal balances remain muted as higher spending is offset by increased tax revenues. However, when displacement severity is broader (including internally displaced persons) GDP effects turn negative, and price impacts differ across goods, with imported staples rising while local cereals stay flat. Labor market outcomes also vary, with employment falling in Burkina Faso but rising in Uganda.

Key takeaways

  • Refugee inflows have limited impact on aggregate GDP, inflation and current‑account balances in the host countries over five years.
  • Higher government spending is largely offset by increased tax revenues, leaving the primary fiscal balance largely unchanged.
  • Displacement raises prices of imported and processed staples while local cereal prices stay flat, and labor effects differ across countries, reducing employment in Burkina Faso but increasing it in Uganda.

Why it matters: Understanding the muted macroeconomic and fiscal effects of large displacement flows helps the markets research team assess inflationary pressures, fiscal sustainability and labor market risks in emerging economies, informing sovereign risk analysis and investment decisions.

Tagged EM: The paper studies forced displacement effects in Burkina Faso, South Sudan and Uganda, all emerging economies in Sub‑Saharan Africa.
